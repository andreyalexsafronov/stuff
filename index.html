ATON’s “For Business” section, rebuilt around the client’s main task

The old section tried to cover every product at once and never explained why a business needs a brokerage account. I ran the research, rebuilt the structure, rewrote the copy and took the page to relaunch through design, legal and compliance. Now the page shows a business its main benefit in seconds and takes a short path to the lead form.

The problem

The page talked about everything, so it said nothing

The stakeholder came with a problem: the page was overloaded and poorly thought out, it brought no leads, and it didn’t work as a product showcase either, because it was never designed as one. It didn’t take deep digging: a quick review made it clear the page had no idea who it was written for.

  • No client in focus

    It listed products instead of answering a business client’s questions.

  • A bad catalogue

    Funds, M&A, IPO advisory and futures sat side by side with no logic.

  • Dead language

    Bureaucratic copy tried to sound expert and got in the way of understanding.

  • Heavy content

    Three embedded videos were hour-long webinar recordings. Nobody watches those on a landing page.

Research

The hypothesis held, and research turned up one more finding

My hypothesis was that the page needed to focus on one product. To test it and look for further insights, I ran 7 interviews with client managers and listened to more than 15 recorded calls with prospective clients, so I heard clients in their own words, not only through a retelling. The hypothesis held, and I found one more factor that shaped the section’s structure.

1

A new business client has one job to start with

Put spare cash somewhere for a very short term, earn interest, keep it simple. That is exchange-traded money market funds, so placing business cash became the page’s lead.

2

The owner decides, but the accountant matters too

Many prospects are already ATON’s personal clients who own a business. The owner decides to open the account, but the accountant will run it, and for them it’s extra work. When the owner sees we’ll support the accountant, the decision gets easier.

Decisions

Every call answered one question: what does the client need to get in touch?

Narrow the audience to companies new to brokerage

Companies planning a bond issue or IPO never arrive through a website. Those deals come through relationships. Investment banking kept one line as a signal of expertise.

Organise the page around client tasks, not products

The four cards at the top open into four sections below, in the same order. Clients find their task immediately.

Lead with placing cash

It’s the first job a new business client has, according to client managers. Long-dated government bonds and active equity trading from the old page didn’t answer it.

Add a block for the accountant

The owner sees the accountant will get guides, accounting materials and answers, which makes the decision easier.

Replace NPS with “would recommend”

Same survey, but a business reader understands it without a definition.

Cut three videos

Hour-long webinar recordings don’t work on a landing page and slowed it down.

Trade-offs

Trade-offs

Not every decision went the way I planned. The first debate was about futures. I felt that for a client just getting to know a brokerage account, futures and hedging were an extra layer of complexity. The stakeholder believed the tool was worth showing even if not everyone would understand it. I gave way, and “Manage risk” stayed on the page as the last of the four tasks.

The second debate was about the block with the company’s credentials. I wanted it right above the lead form, so the rating and track record would settle final doubts at the moment of decision. The stakeholder insisted on the top of the page: in their experience, treasurers and accountants look for those numbers first, and they decide whether to read on. The stakeholder knew this audience better than I did, so the block stayed at the top.

The longest debate was with legal. They read some lines as promising guaranteed returns and proposed replacing many plain phrases with formally correct jargon. I went through the wording line by line and kept almost all of it, with footnotes added. The page stayed clear to an ordinary person, and legal accuracy didn’t suffer.
